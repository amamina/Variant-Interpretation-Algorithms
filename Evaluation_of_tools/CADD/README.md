**Combined annotation-dependent depletion** is a tool that scores the deleteriousness of SNVs, MNVs and indels. CADD operates by comparing the annotations of derived alleles that are nearly or completely fixed in humans to those of simulated variants. Since natural selection eliminates deleterious mutations that reduce organism fitness, such deleterious variants are less common in fixed alleles but remain present in simulated data (Kircher et al., 2014). 

**CADD therefore evaluated deleteriousness**, a trait that is highly associated with both pathogenicity and molecular functionality. A key advantage of deleterious matrices over functionality or pathogenicity is that it could be measured systematically across the entire genome, unlike the latter that rely on limited set of well characterized genetic or experimental mutations and are prone to ascertainment biases. Furthermore the selective pressure on genetic variants is influenced by their overall impact on phenotypically relevant traits rather than any single molecular or phenotypic outcome. Consequently measure of deleteriousness can provide a comprehensive, genome wide assessment of variant effect that is data driven, functionally broad, and organismally relevant (Cooper et al., 2005).

**CADD analyses variations between human genome and the inferred human-chimpanzee ancestral genome** where humans possess a DAF of ≥ 95% (comprising 14.9 million SNVs and 1.7 million indels). The majority of these alleles are fully established in human lineage only fewer than 5% exist as nearly fixed polymorphism in 1000 Genome Project dataset. Secondly to create a comparable set of de novo mutations an empirical model of sequence evolution that accounts for CpG dinucleotide-specific mutations rate was used, with local mutations rate estimated on a 1Mb scale. Both mutation rate and indel size distribution were derived from six-way primate genome alignments (Paten et al., 2008).

**Annotation data** was gathered from Ensembl VEP tool (McLaren et al., 2010), USCS Genome Browser (Meyer et al., 2012) and Encode project (Consortium, 2012). These annotations covered multiple data range including conservation metrics (GERP, PhyloP, PhastCons), protein level score (Grantham, SIFT, PolyPhen), regulatory information (transcription factor binding sits, genomic regions of DNase I hypersensitivity), transcript level information (exon-intron boundary distance or gene expression level). The final annotation dataset comprised 29.4 million variants with 63 different annotations with an equal split between half fixed or nearly fixed (observed) and de novo mutations (simulated) (Kircher et al., 2014).

**This approach was tested by ****univariate models **that compares observed and simulated variants treating each of 63 annotations as single predictor. Majority of these models showed strong predictive power in distinguishing observed from simulated variants aligning with expectations. Notably non-sense variants showed approximately a 20 fold depletion, while missense variants exhibited 2-fold depletion, also these nonsense or missense mutation showed more depletion in near the start site of coding DNA than those occurring at ends, with no depletion found in intergenic variants. Variants positioned within 20 nucleotides of splice junction particularly with 2 nucleotides also showed significant depletion. On a genome wide scale conservation matrics proved to be the most robust annotations.

**A support vector machine (SVM) with linear kernel **was trained using features derived from 63 annotations (Franc & Sonnenburg, 2009). Ten separate models were trained on observed and simulated variant data. These models were highly correlated showing Spearman correlations > 0.99%. The final model was used to assign C-score to 8.6 billion SNVs, in human genome (GRCH37). To simplify these interpretation scaled C score (Phred-like C score) was created ranging from 1 to 99 (Ewing & Green, 1998). Variants in the top 10% that were least likely to be harmful in humans get a C10 or higher, while those in the top 1% or 0.1% assigned a score of ≥C20 or ≥C30.

**Genome wide properties of C-score **was assessed by determining the functional impact of possible substitutions, derived from observed and simulated variant differences to avoid ascertainment bias. Nonsense variants had the highest C-score (median of 37). Followed by missense and splice variants (median of 15), while intergenic variants had the lowest score (median of 2).

**Advantages of using C-Score** is that nonsense mutations are treated uniformly in disease studies actually had a lower C-score in olfactory receptor genes compared to other genes. While those in previously identified essential genes had higher score. This highlights the ability of C-score to capture detailed functional insights both across and within mutation categories. This distinction is either absent or less pronounced in other scoring methods due to missing data (e.g conservation metrics which cannot distinguish between nonsense and missense mutations at the same site).

**CADD’s ability to prioritize functionally significant and disease associated genetic variation across multiple context**. It distinguishes pathogenic from benign variants in Kabuki syndrome (KMT2D gene) and β-Thalassemia (HBB gene) while correlating with disease severity. CADD also outperforms other methods in identifying ClinVar pathogenic variants, TP53 cancer mutation and regulatory variants affecting gene expression. As it provides a ranking rather than a prediction, in Ensembl genome browser score above 30 is suggested to be likely-deleterious, while a score below it is considered as benign (Rentzsch et al., 2019).
